# Weight Class Can Beat MenuFit on Trust

MenuFit is a hard-paywalled "decide before you order" recommender, not a calorie tracker, and as of October 2026 it is the runaway leader of its niche: a 25-to-34-screen quiz sets Mifflin–St Jeor targets, Nutritionix supplies chain nutrition, and OpenAI assembles 0–100-scored meal combinations and writes the reasons, a formula that reportedly passed **$1M a month in revenue after store fees** within a year of its August 2025 launch, powered by founder-led fast-food videos and roughly 400 live Meta ads. Its weakness is trust, not reach: a **4.77-star average from 54,507 US ratings** sits on top of written reviews averaging about **2.6–2.7 stars**, dominated by trial and billing anger, understated calories and "macro-gamed" picks such as burger patties with the buns thrown away scored "98 Excellent". At least **41 imitators** have launched since, holding about 13% of MenuFit's ratings between them, and almost none pairs ranked picks with a diary that knows what the user has left today, which is the opening for Weight Class. The obstacle is licensing, not engineering. FatSecret, the source behind Weight Class's built-but-dark restaurant feature, forbids using its data for "diet, nutrition or health advice, guidance", so it can show and log numbers but never rank them; Nutritionix permits guidance but is the wrong purchase today, because its $499-a-month Starter plan bans storing even item IDs and the plans that allow keeping logged meals cost **$11,988 a year for 1,000 monthly users** or a custom quote from about $22,200. The better build is a first-party table of the biggest chains' published nutrition (facts carry no copyright, and the top 50 chains take 61% of restaurant spending), ranked by deterministic code, with AI confined to reading menus, estimating independent-restaurant dishes (where frontier models miss calories by roughly **30–36%** and underestimate large plates) and wording reasons. The binding risks are allergen "safe" labels, "guilt-free" framing for people prone to disordered eating, unsubstantiated accuracy claims, sharing inferred health data with AI vendors without separate consent, and Apple's new guideline on dormant features, which means ranking must ship visibly in a reviewed build rather than behind a server flag.

## MenuFit turns a quiz and fast-food videos into $1M a month

### A recommender wrapped around a hard paywall

MenuFit, sold by Kosco Digital LLC of Palm Beach, Florida, does one job: telling people what to order before they eat. A long onboarding quiz collects goal, body metrics, how often they eat out, favourite chains, liked and disliked foods and allergies; ScreensDesign counts **34 steps** ([ScreensDesign](https://screensdesign.com/showcase/menufit-healthy-eating-out)) and the founder "about 25 screens" ([36Kr](https://eu.36kr.com/zh/p/4024343832268928)), followed by sign-up and a card-required free trial, with at most "a sneak peek at a limited selection of meals" before paying ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.myhealthymenu.app&hl=en_US&gl=US)). Paying users pick a nearby or searched restaurant and see meal combinations, each carrying a 0–100 badge such as "100 EXCELLENT" with calories and macros, plus a "Healthify" swap tool, an AI chat with voice input and, since a September 2026 redesign, a map ([ScreensDesign](https://screensdesign.com/apps/menufit-healthy-eating-out/); [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/menufit-healthy-eating-out/id6746144481)). None of the sources shows a food diary, weight tracking or a Health integration, so MenuFit cannot know what a user has already eaten that day.

The engine, as the company described it to 36Kr, is **Nutritionix for nutrition data, Mifflin–St Jeor for energy needs, and OpenAI, which "combines menu items and generates recommendation reasons from the calculations and user preferences"** ([36Kr](https://eu.36kr.com/zh/p/4024343832268928)). The terms widen the sources to "restaurants, third-party databases, publicly available sources, automated systems, artificial intelligence or estimates" and concede that MenuFit "cannot guarantee that any recommendation is free of a particular ingredient or allergen" ([MenuFit Terms](https://menufit.com/terms-and-conditions/)). The headline coverage claim, "any restaurant in the world with 22.3 Million+ locations" ([menufit.com](https://menufit.com/)), dwarfs Nutritionix's **202,837 items from 860 restaurants** ([Nutritionix](https://www.nutritionix.com/database)) and even the 10.56 million food-and-drink places in Overture's full September 2026 release, 1.59 million of them in the US ([Overture release files](https://overturemaps-us-west-2.s3.amazonaws.com/?list-type=2&prefix=release/2026-09-23.1/theme=places/type=place/)); no provider publishes a 22.3 million figure, so the places source is unconfirmed. The likeliest reading is that chains get database values and everything else gets AI estimates from menu text, which fits the "Restaurant Data Confidence Meter" added in May 2026 and the promise that the app "works even if the menu has no calories or macros listed" ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/menufit-healthy-eating-out/id6746144481)). MenuFit, in short, already runs the database-plus-AI hybrid Weight Class is weighing, but hides the seam between published and guessed numbers.

Pricing is tested hard. MenuFit launched at **$9.99 a month or $19.99 a year** ([Social Growth Engineers](https://www.socialgrowthengineers.com/fast-food-swaps-to-60k-mrr-in-two-months)); by October 2026 the US App Store listed **ten "MenuFit Premium" products from $9.99 to $48.00** ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/menufit-healthy-eating-out/id6746144481)), the same slots that in May carried names like "Zentrox Plan" and "Apex Plan" ([AppPricingLab](https://apppricinglab.com/iap/apple/6746144481)), the signature of continuous paywall experiments. The paywall preselects the yearly plan under a free-trial banner ([ScreensDesign](https://screensdesign.com/apps/menufit-healthy-eating-out/)), and reviewers describe 3-, 7- and 14-day trials. The founder says 99.8% of users finish the quiz and 40–45% of them start a trial or pay, figures 36Kr notes it did not verify ([36Kr](https://eu.36kr.com/zh/p/4024343832268928)).

### Distribution, not data, is the moat

The commercial record is strong, though every revenue figure is a third-party estimate or a founder claim. MenuFit reached **$60K in monthly recurring revenue two months after launch** ([Social Growth Engineers](https://www.socialgrowthengineers.com/fast-food-swaps-to-60k-mrr-in-two-months)), and by August 2026 its monthly revenue after platform fees passed **$1M**, ninth worldwide in AppMagic's weight and calorie-tracking tag, a ranking 36Kr says "may have omissions" ([36Kr](https://eu.36kr.com/zh/p/4024343832268928)). iOS passed one million downloads in under a year ([mwm.ai](https://mwm.ai/apps/menufit-healthy-eating-out/6746144481)), Android sits in the 100K+ bucket with about 299,000 installs by Google's embedded count ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.myhealthymenu.app&hl=en_US&gl=US)), and on 18 October 2026 the app ranked **#13 in US Health & Fitness with 4.77 stars from 54,507 ratings** ([iTunes Lookup](https://itunes.apple.com/lookup?id=6746144481&country=us); [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/menufit-healthy-eating-out/id6746144481)). Canada, the UK and Australia add only about 1,600 ratings between them, so this is a US business ([iTunes Lookup, Canada](https://itunes.apple.com/lookup?id=6746144481&country=ca); [UK](https://itunes.apple.com/lookup?id=6746144481&country=gb); [Australia](https://itunes.apple.com/lookup?id=6746144481&country=au)), and the site's "1.5 million happy users" is credible only as cumulative downloads. Behind it stands founder Cole Kosco, a former college athlete turned fitness coach, whose development bill "far exceeded" a $15,000 estimate and who has disclosed no outside funding ([36Kr](https://eu.36kr.com/en/p/4024343832268928)).

Growth came from content before code. Kosco's May 2025 Chipotle comparison drew **2.7 million views**, and viewers who commented "Food" received a chain-macros PDF by automated DM, a funnel that now runs from DM to a reviews page to the quiz to the paywall ([36Kr](https://eu.36kr.com/zh/p/4024343832268928); [Social Growth Engineers](https://www.socialgrowthengineers.com/fast-food-swaps-to-60k-mrr-in-two-months)). Paid Meta ads began in November 2025, fed by a contract-free creator programme that pays **$20 for a first approved video and 10% of the ad spend behind any winner** ([36Kr](https://eu.36kr.com/en/p/4024343832268928)); the Ad Library showed about **413 active US ads** on 18 October 2026, with new batches created on 17 and 18 October ([Meta Ad Library](https://www.facebook.com/ads/library/?active_status=all&ad_type=all&country=US&view_all_page_id=760146920523040)). The copy courts two audiences at once, "cutting or bulking" gym-goers and dieters promised "Finally Eat Out Guilt-Free" ([menufit.com](https://menufit.com/)), and borrows wellness trends such as a "Seed Oil Free Restaurants" filter ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/menufit-healthy-eating-out/id6746144481)). Rivals now publish "MenuFit alternative" pages to harvest its brand searches ([MenuScout](https://menuscout.app/menufit-alternative/); [LeanBites](https://leanbites.app/blog/leanbites-vs-menufit-macros-eating-out)).

### Written reviews average under three stars

Behind the star average sits a different verdict. Written reviews average **2.6 across 1,131 on iOS** ([mwm.ai](https://mwm.ai/apps/menufit-healthy-eating-out/6746144481)) and **2.73 across 893 from the past year** in 36Kr's count ([36Kr](https://eu.36kr.com/zh/p/4024343832268928)); of the 500 most recent, **61% are one or two stars and 43% mention the paywall, trial or billing** ([App Store review feed](https://itunes.apple.com/us/rss/customerreviews/page=1/id=6746144481/sortby=mostrecent/json)). The gap most plausibly comes from a rating prompt placed inside onboarding, before the paywall and before any real use ([ScreensDesign](https://screensdesign.com/apps/menufit-healthy-eating-out/)), a tactic Weight Class's own rules forbid ([PLAN.md](../PLAN.md)). The substantive complaints map onto the architecture. Numbers run low even at chains: one user measured MenuFit **28% under at Taziki's and 40% under at McDonald's** against the chains' own websites ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/menufit-healthy-eating-out/id6746144481)), and 36Kr's test found a LongHorn sirloin-and-shrimp plate shown at **400 kcal that the official menu puts near 860** once its rice and garlic butter are counted ([36Kr](https://eu.36kr.com/zh/p/4024343832268928)). Picks are gamed: a McDonald's order stacking a Big Mac, a McDouble and two extra patties while "telling me to throw away all the buns and vegetables" scored **"98 Excellent"**, a craving for eggs produced "an egg dish WITHOUT THE EGGS", and changing weight goals "barely seems to affect the recommended calories or macros at all" ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/menufit-healthy-eating-out/id6746144481)); another user found disliked foods in every recommendation ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.myhealthymenu.app&hl=en_US&gl=US)). These are the failure modes of letting a language model assemble orders against macro targets without realism constraints.

The rest of the experience compounds the problem. Support replies are slow and the Play listing's support address is a Gmail account, reviewers call the AI-generated restaurant logos "slop", and users say the September 2026 redesign removed per-item macros and favourites, with version 1.0.52 a week later promising calories and macros "on all restaurant menus" again ([mwm.ai](https://mwm.ai/apps/menufit-healthy-eating-out/6746144481); [Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.myhealthymenu.app&hl=en_US&gl=US); [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/menufit-healthy-eating-out/id6746144481)). The privacy disclosures contradict each other: the iOS label declares tracking and precise location but no health data, Google Play's form says "No data collected", and the privacy policy describes collecting body metrics, allergies and precise geolocation ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/menufit-healthy-eating-out/id6746144481); [Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.myhealthymenu.app&hl=en_US&gl=US); [Privacy policy](https://menufit.com/privacy-policy/)). The founder also appears among the site's testimonials as a "Nutrition Coach" without saying he owns the company ([menufit.com](https://menufit.com/)), an endorsement-disclosure problem worth not copying. MenuFit has proved the demand and the distribution; its product and its compliance are both beatable.

## Forty-one imitators share 13% of MenuFit's ratings

The market has split into three camps, and none owns the whole job. Incumbent trackers treat restaurants as logging databases: MyFitnessPal advertises "20.5M+ foods, including restaurant dishes" ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/myfitnesspal-calorie-counter/id341232718)), WeightWatchers "450+ restaurants" ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/weightwatchers-program/id331308914)) and Nutritionix Track "760+ US restaurant chain menus" with no ranking ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/nutritionix-track/id1061691342)). Only **MyNetDiary** sells a real pre-order tool, an "AI Restaurant Menu Scan: find the best options when eating out" inside a **$14.99 Premium Plus** tier ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/calorie-counter-mynetdiary/id287529757)). Strikingly, users of two of the biggest incumbents report losing restaurant browsing in 2026: "They got rid of restaurant menus" in MyFitnessPal reviews from September ([MFP review feed](https://itunes.apple.com/us/rss/customerreviews/page=1/id=341232718/sortby=mostrecent/json)) and "you used to be able to look foods up by restaurant, and that feature is gone" for Lose It! ([Lose It! review feed](https://itunes.apple.com/us/rss/customerreviews/page=1/id=297368629/sortby=mostrecent/json)), though neither company has confirmed a removal. AI photo trackers help only after the meal. Cal AI, which MyFitnessPal bought in spring 2026 ([9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2026/04/21/popular-calorie-tracker-briefly-pulled-from-app-store-over-iap-and-billing-violations/)), never mentions restaurants in its listing, and its reviewers call its restaurant data "weak" ([Cal AI review feed](https://itunes.apple.com/us/rss/customerreviews/page=1/id=6480417616/sortby=mostrecent/json)); MacroFactor users praise its AI for restaurant meals and ask for chain menus ([MacroFactor review feed](https://itunes.apple.com/us/rss/customerreviews/page=1/id=1553503471/sortby=mostrecent/json)).

The third camp is MenuFit's wave. Six App Store searches on 18 October 2026 found **41 eat-out apps launched between September 2025 and October 2026, holding 6,904 ratings between them against MenuFit's 54,507**; only eight have 100 ratings or more and 13 have none, and the count is a floor because each search returns at most 25 apps ([App Store search](https://itunes.apple.com/search?term=healthy+eating+out&entity=software&country=us); [search](https://itunes.apple.com/search?term=restaurant+calories&entity=software&country=us); [search](https://itunes.apple.com/search?term=menu+scanner+calories&entity=software&country=us)). Most copy one template (goal profile, ranked menu with a fit score, swaps, a menu-photo scanner) and most paywall first. General AI hovers over all of them: "Chat gbt can do this" recurs as an objection in paid-app reviews ([LeanBites review feed](https://itunes.apple.com/us/rss/customerreviews/page=1/id=6754943623/sortby=mostrecent/json)), OpenAI's ChatGPT Health launched in January 2026 with MyFitnessPal data plugged in ([GSMArena](https://www.gsmarena.com/openai_launches_chatgpt_health_with_apple_health_and_myfitnesspal_integration-news-71006.php)), and Yelp's Menu Vision overlays dish photos and reviews on a live menu but shows no calories ([Yelp Engineering](https://engineeringblog.yelp.com/2026/08/building-menu-vision-real-time-dish-recognition.html)). Reddit could not be read with the available tools, so community sentiment remains a gap.

The newest entrants show where the category is heading. MacroBite ranks its compiled chain meals "against the calories and macros you have left today", and 2nd Menu promises "exact chain-published nutrition where it exists" with everything else labelled as an estimate, so honest sourcing is turning into table stakes. What stays rare is ranking against today's remaining budget and logging into a real diary, which is structurally hard for a standalone recommender and native to a tracker. Users ask for precisely that: "it would be ten times better if you had an option to set daily calories and then add the meal… That way it's all in the same app" ([What2Eat review feed](https://itunes.apple.com/us/rss/customerreviews/page=1/id=6752793894/sortby=mostrecent/json)).

The underlying problem is large, and menu labels barely dent it. Food away from home took **56.3% of US food spending in 2025, $1.41 trillion** ([USDA ERS](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/114212)). Independent-restaurant meals averaged **1,205 kcal** by bomb calorimetry ([PMC5746190](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5746190/)) and US full-service meals **1,362 kcal** ([PMC6290458](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6290458/)), so one dinner can consume most of a 1,500–1,800 kcal weight-loss budget. At one 104-restaurant fast-food franchise, mandatory calorie labels trimmed about 60 kcal per transaction at first and 23 after a year ([BMJ](https://www.bmj.com/content/367/bmj.l5837.full.pdf)), and only about half of adults notice them ([USDA ERS Amber Waves](https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2024/august/calorie-labels-at-restaurants-half-of-adults-notice-but-some-more-than-others)). Coverage is lopsided: more than **7 in 10 US restaurants are single-unit operations** ([National Restaurant Association](https://go.restaurant.org/rs/078-ZLA-461/images/2024-soi-pocketbook.pdf)), while **the top 50 chains take 61% of restaurant spending from 24% of locations** ([Circana](https://www.circana.com/post/circana-announces-top-50-u-s-restaurants-for-2025)). A chain table therefore covers most of the dollars but a minority of the doors, and menu reading is the only route to the rest.

GLP-1 users, a core Weight Class audience, change the brief. In Toast's August 2026 survey of 850 US GLP-1 users who eat out weekly, **62% order less food, 87% would order a smaller-portion version of a regular item, 44% have ordered an appetizer as a main, and 89% say listed protein grams would sway where they eat** ([Toast](https://pos.toasttab.com/blog/data/glp-1-restaurant-habits)); Circana finds their restaurant visits stable in the first year and a GLP-1 user in 23% of households ([Circana](https://www.circana.com/post/glp-1-users-aren-t-ditching-restaurants-but-their-ordering-habits-are-changing-new-circana-researc); [Nation's Restaurant News](https://www.nrn.com/restaurant-insights/glp-1-users-spend-more-at-restaurants-despite-cutting-back-on-alcohol-and-retail-food-purchases)). Their job is fitting a small appetite and a protein target, which only Menu Order AI's satiety ratio and MacroBite's GLP-1 category address. Price is no obstacle: Weight Class Premium at **$7.99 a month or $39.99 a year with a one-month trial** ([PLAN.md](../PLAN.md)) sits inside the eat-out apps' range while also being a full tracker, against MyFitnessPal Premium at $79.99 a year ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/myfitnesspal-calorie-counter/id341232718)). And the category's loudest complaint is billing: LeanBites' 161 most recent written reviews are **77% one or two stars, with 55% about the paywall, trial or billing** ([LeanBites review feed](https://itunes.apple.com/us/rss/customerreviews/page=1/id=6754943623/sortby=mostrecent/json)).

## FatSecret forbids ranking, and Nutritionix prices it out of reach

Weight Class's restaurant feature (search chains, build a plate, see it against what is left today, log it or save it) is finished but switched off in production ([PLAN.md](../PLAN.md)), and its data source cannot carry a MenuFit-style feature. FatSecret's terms bar using the API "to provide diet, nutrition or health advice, guidance or diagnosis" (§1.7(iii)), let only listed IDs such as `food_id` and `serving_id` be kept indefinitely, and require everything else to be re-requested within 24 hours (§1.5) ([FatSecret terms](https://platform.fatsecret.com/terms); [FatSecret storable data](https://platform.fatsecret.com/docs/guides/storable-data)). Ranked "best fits", "best picks" or healthy badges computed from FatSecret values read as guidance however they are computed, which is why the plan already promises the app will show numbers and totals but "never 'best picks', healthy labels or rankings" ([PLAN.md](../PLAN.md)). Plain numbers and user-built plates remain fine. Two details belong in the pending letter to FatSecret: its editions page ticks "Caching" for Premier Free, which conflicts with the 24-hour rule ([FatSecret editions](https://platform.fatsecret.com/api-editions)), and the advice ban sits in the general terms every edition accepts, so paying for Premier does not lift it unless FatSecret agrees in writing. FatSecret values also cannot seed a first-party table, because copying them breaks the same storage clause.

Nutritionix is the mirror image: the licence fits and the price does not. Its §1 anticipates "Applications that provide dietary guidance to users" and asks only for a risk notice in the app's own terms, and the full agreement contains no clause on ranking, scores, labels or generative AI ([Nutritionix License Agreement](https://developer.nutritionix.com/signup)). The constraints are storage and money. §14 forbids caching "brand names, item names, and Nutritionix IDs" unless agreed in writing, and the pricing page allows caching "user food log data for historical reference purpose only" solely on the MVP and Unicorn plans ([Nutritionix pricing](https://www.nutritionix.com/api)). §13 requires every call to be "initiated by an end-user, and not by an automated server process", §2 makes data "derived from the Nutritionix Service" Nutritionix's property, and §9 obliges a departing licensee to destroy all "Nutritionix User Data" ([Nutritionix License Agreement](https://developer.nutritionix.com/signup)), a clause that would reach into users' logged meal histories. Plans are billed annually: **Starter costs $499 a month for 200 monthly active users with no caching and no natural-language engine, MVP $999 for 1,000, and Unicorn starts at $1,850**, while freemium apps are told to ask for custom pricing ([Nutritionix pricing](https://www.nutritionix.com/api)).

**Verdict: do not use Nutritionix now.** Starter is unworkable for a diary, since a logged restaurant meal could not even keep its item ID. Because Nutritionix counts as active only users whose actions call its API, MVP's 1,000-user cap would cover early restaurant users, but at **$11,988 a year** for a feature that has not yet proved its pull; growth beyond that, or the freemium label that fits Weight Class, means a custom quote starting near **$22,200 a year** ([Nutritionix pricing](https://www.nutritionix.com/api)). The coverage bought is chains only, 202,837 items from 860 restaurants and US-first ([Nutritionix database](https://www.nutritionix.com/database)), and a first-party table of the top 50 chains would cover most of the same restaurant spending for staff time instead of licence fees. Nutritionix becomes worth reopening once restaurant usage makes $22,000 a year small, and only on an order form that grants indefinite storage of logged items with all nutrients, ranking against a user's remaining macros, display beside AI estimates labelled by source, and relief from §2's derived-data claim and §9's purge. Its unpriced bulk licence, delivered for local storage "for unlimited use", is the one Nutritionix route without the caching ban and worth a quote ([Nutritionix database](https://www.nutritionix.com/database)). The "Nutritionix Platform Policy" that §4 and §12 incorporate could not be found anywhere and must be read before signing.

Edamam is the cheapest licensed option with room to rank, but it lets subscribers cache only calories, protein, net carbs and fat in the user's account ([Edamam](https://developer.edamam.com/food-database-api)), so a logged restaurant meal would lose the sugar and sodium that Weight Class's sugar note relies on, which is why the plan passed on it ([PLAN.md](../PLAN.md)). The places providers sort cleanly. Yelp bans ranking its content, commercial use without consent and feeding it into "any Generative AI Model" ([Yelp API Terms](https://terms.yelp.com/developers/api_terms/20260922_en_us/)); Google forbids saving "business names, addresses" and any use "in a listings or directory service" ([Google Maps Platform Terms](https://cloud.google.com/maps-platform/terms)) and exposes no menu items at all ([Place reference](https://developers.google.com/maps/documentation/places/web-service/reference/rest/v1/places)); Apple allows stored Place IDs but requires an Apple map beside surfaced results and forbids derived databases ([Apple DPLA](https://developer.apple.com/support/terms/apple-developer-program-license-agreement/)); and Overture's places theme ships under CDLA-Permissive-2.0 and Apache 2.0 with no share-alike duty ([Overture](https://docs.overturemaps.org/guides/places/)). For independent restaurants, licensed nutrition does not exist. OpenMenu sells 550,000+ menus as a $2,499 national dataset, but no licence terms could be found and its sample data dates from 2010, and MealMe gates everything behind a sales form ([OpenMenu](https://www.openmenu.com/pricing); [OpenMenu developers](https://www.openmenu.com/developers); [MealMe](https://www.mealme.ai/)).

The first-party route rests on settled law. "That there can be no valid copyright in facts is universally understood," and a later compiler "remains free to use the facts contained in another's publication" so long as it does not copy the selection and arrangement ([Feist v. Rural](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/499/340)); the US has no EU-style database right ([Emory Libraries](https://libraries.emory.edu/research/copyright/copyright-data)). Federal rules guarantee the facts exist: every chain with **20 or more locations** must have written nutrition information covering 11 nutrients, sugars and sodium included, and "menus on the Internet" count as menus ([21 CFR 101.11](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/101.11)). The residual risk is contractual, from website terms against scraping, which a 2024 district ruling held preempted by copyright law in a decision that binds no other court ([Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2024/05/district-court-adopts-broad-view)). Human-checked transcription from each chain's own nutrition guide into Weight Class's schema, with a source link and check date per item and no copied PDFs, layouts or logos, keeps that risk low. One wrinkle: Nutritionix says its dietitians "manage the nutrition pages on major restaurants' websites" ([Nutritionix database](https://www.nutritionix.com/database)), so where a chain's page is Nutritionix-hosted, the chain's own PDF is the safer source.

## AI estimates miss by a third, so code should rank and AI should read

No peer-reviewed study yet tests language models on US chain menu text against the chains' published nutrition, so the evidence comes from plate photos, meal descriptions and packaged meals. It is consistent all the same.

|---|---|---|

| Plate photo only: ChatGPT-5 (195 dishes) | Calorie MAPE 30.5% (123 kcal); fat 52.9% | [PMC12655113](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12655113/) |

| Same, plus an ingredient list with amounts | 13.9% | [PMC12655113](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12655113/) |

| Packaged meals against labels (8 meals) | Dietitians 99.5% of label calories; chatbots 71–127%; fat up to 201% | [PMC12526241](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12526241/) |

The direction of the error matters more than its size. Models **underestimate more as portions grow**, with regression slopes of −0.34 to −0.45 ([PMC12513282](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513282/)) and large meals judged at 530 g when they weighed 798 g ([PMC11858203](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11858203/)). Restaurant plates are the largest most people eat, so unguarded estimates make heavy dishes look as if they fit, the same direction as MenuFit's LongHorn plate and the complaint that Order Fit "underestimates… you might struggle and overeat 400-800 calories a day" ([Order Fit review feed](https://itunes.apple.com/us/rss/customerreviews/page=1/id=6757307450/sortby=mostrecent/json)). Fat and protein are the weakest estimates, and the authors of the 52-photo study call the models "not yet suitable for precise dietary assessment in clinical or athletic populations" ([PMC12513282](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513282/)), a line that matters for a brand called Weight Class whose users may be making weight. Two rules follow. For chains, the published number should win, even though labels themselves drift beyond ±20% for many items. For independents, the honest claim is "a reasonable estimate": on text, models perform about as well as nutritionists, and neither is a gold standard.

Information beats prompt cleverness. Adding non-visual facts cut ChatGPT-5's calorie error from 30.5% to 24.4%, and an ingredient list cut it to 13.9% ([PMC12655113](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12655113/)); Weight Class already asks one tap-to-answer question about portion, oil or butter on photo scans ([PLAN.md](../PLAN.md)), and the same mechanic fits menu items ("grilled or fried?", "side or entrée size?"). Step-by-step reasoning helps multi-item meals, while retrieval from a nutrient database "does not consistently help" ([NutriBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.12843v2)); breaking a dish into components linked to FNDDS codes improves portion estimates and leaves an auditable record even though it no longer beats a modern model's direct guess overall ([Open-KNEAD preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12911)). Models' stated confidence runs high ([Xiong et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.13063)), so ranges should come from measured error per dish category rather than from the self-reported confidence Weight Class saves on each meal today. Model choice moves results more than prompts do, with an R² of 0.23 for Claude Haiku against 0.60 for Sonnet on packaged foods ([Nutrients 2026](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/18/12/2017/htm)), and one food app reports lifting its share of estimates within 10% from 17% to 76% through a golden test set and few-shot examples, a vendor case rather than a study ([ZenML](https://www.zenml.io/llmops-database/improving-llm-food-tracking-accuracy-through-systematic-evaluation-and-few-shot-learning)). A first-party chain table fills Weight Class's missing benchmark: thousands of items with published values make a ready golden set for the model benchmark still open in the plan ([PLAN.md](../PLAN.md)).

**Feeding database numbers to an AI that calculates the recommendations is the wrong division of labour.** MenuFit's design, in which OpenAI "combines menu items" from the calculations, produced its worst reviews: bunless patty stacks, egg dishes without eggs, goals that barely move the picks. A scoring function in code couples directly to the remaining budget, returns the same answer twice, runs instantly at no cost and can be published, which matters because Apple's guideline 1.4.1 requires apps to "clearly disclose data and methodology to support accuracy claims relating to health measurements" ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Licensing points the same way: FatSecret data cannot drive guidance by any method, Nutritionix claims anything "derived" from its service and bars automated server-side calls ([Nutritionix License Agreement](https://developer.nutritionix.com/signup)), Yelp forbids generative-AI ingestion outright ([Yelp API Terms](https://terms.yelp.com/developers/api_terms/20260922_en_us/)), and Google's terms bar creating content from its Maps data, with a carve-out for language models only through its Grounding Lite product and its source links ([Google Maps Platform Terms](https://cloud.google.com/maps-platform/terms); [Google service terms](https://cloud.google.com/maps-platform/terms/maps-service-terms)). Privacy does too: ranking on Weight Class's server keeps the user's goals, remaining macros and allergy list away from the AI vendor, a share that Washington's health-data law would otherwise require separate consent for. The strongest case for an AI ranker is that it understands messy wishes like "craving eggs, no bun". The answer is to let the model translate such wishes into structured constraints, let code rank, and let the model write a one-line reason from a template filled with the computed numbers, never the numbers themselves. That leaves the AI four jobs: reading menus, estimating components for dishes without published values, parsing preferences, and wording.

The ranking should encode what MenuFit's lacks. Hard filters remove dislikes and flag, never clear, allergens. A fit score measures closeness to a band around the remaining calories and protein and penalises both overshoot and implausibly small meals, so "lowest calories" never wins. Realism rules forbid stripping a dish's core components, cap items per order and set a meal-sized calorie floor. GLP-1 mode breaks ties on protein per 100 kcal and suggests half portions or an appetizer as the main. Estimated dishes rank on the upper part of their range, so uncertainty cannot sneak a heavy plate into "fits", and where ranges overlap the app shows a group of good fits instead of a false one-two-three. Labels such as "720 kcal · from the restaurant" and "650–850 kcal · AI estimate" are backed by experiments showing that numeric ranges lower confidence in the number but not trust in the source, while vague caveats lower both ([Winton Centre](https://wintoncentre.maths.cam.ac.uk/news/being-upfront-about-your-uncertainty-doesnt-necessarily-mean-you-lose-your-audiences-trust-new-paper-published); [Kerr et al.](https://doaj.org/article/f976256fe94e402888b221aa79257f77)).

## Allergens, eating disorders and Apple's newest rule set the guardrails

No app can declare a restaurant dish safe for an allergy. The FDA's 2022 Food Code puts written allergen notice on food establishments, and only where states adopt it ([Bradley](https://www.bradley.com/insights/publications/2023/03/key-takeaways-from-the-2022-fda-food-code)); FARE tells diners to raise allergies with staff every time and to ask about cross-contact ([FARE](https://www.foodallergy.org/sites/default/files/2020-11/FARE_DiningOutWFA-110920.pdf)); and no study has measured how well language models spot allergens in dishes, while the same chatbot's detail-level estimates swing widely between runs, with sodium varying by a coefficient of variation up to 69% ([PMC12526241](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12526241/)). MenuFit's terms disclaim allergen safety, and a rival's allergy filter is "a hit or miss" ([What2Eat review feed](https://itunes.apple.com/us/rss/customerreviews/page=1/id=6752793894/sortby=mostrecent/json)). Weight Class should flag "may contain peanut, ask staff", never label a dish "safe" or "allergen-free", never imply an unflagged dish is clear, and match allergens on its own server against dish text instead of sending the allergy list to the AI.

Calorie-forward design carries documented harm for a minority. In a 2026 survey of 1,001 people with disordered eating in England, **half said calorie labels worsen their symptoms**, and optional labels were the most popular policy, ranked in the top three by 63.6% ([UCL](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2026/apr/calorie-labels-helpful-people-binge-eating-disorders)); a 38-study review links diet-app use with disordered eating, though not causally ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39671845/)); and NEDA pulled its "Tessa" chatbot in 2023 after it told users to keep a 500–1,000 kcal daily deficit ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2023/06/08/1181131532/eating-disorder-helpline-takes-down-chatbot-after-it-gave-weight-loss-advice)). MenuFit's "Finally Eat Out Guilt-Free" is the moral framing to avoid. Weight Class already hides every number in the restaurant screens under calm mode, links a helpline and excludes people with an eating-disorder history from its target audience ([PLAN.md](../PLAN.md)); ranking adds three rules of its own: never reward the lowest-calorie option, replace the ranking with a neutral message once the day's budget is spent, and generate reasons from templates instead of open chat.

Federal consumer-protection law targets the marketing. The FTC says "there is no AI exemption from the laws on the books" ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/09/ftc-announces-crackdown-deceptive-ai-claims-schemes)) and in 2025 finalised an order against Workado, whose AI detector claimed 98% accuracy but tested at 53% ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/08/ftc-approves-final-order-against-workado-llc-which-misrepresented-accuracy-its-artificial)). The current Commission has rolled some AI theories back, setting aside the Rytr order in December 2025 ([Covington](https://www.insideprivacy.com/united-states/federal-trade-commission/ftc-sets-aside-rytr-final-order-pursuant-to-white-house-ai-action-plan/)), but its July 2026 proposed policy statement, not yet final, says accuracy marketing without "adequate disclaimers or qualifications" can deceive and that disclaimers cannot hide in terms of service ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/07/2026-13628/policy-statement-concerning-the-suppression-of-accuracy-in-artificial-intelligence-systems)). Health-benefit claims still need "competent and reliable scientific evidence", generally randomised trials ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)). That rules out "accurate", "exact", "verified", "dietitian-approved" and "eat out and still lose weight", and puts the "AI estimate" label on the number rather than in the terms. The amended Health Breach Notification Rule covers diet and fitness apps and counts an **unauthorised disclosure** as a breach ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/04/ftc-finalizes-changes-health-breach-notification-rule)), so menu text, photos, restaurant identity and location must join the AI consent before they reach OpenRouter or OpenAI; whether the rule reaches Weight Class at all is a question for counsel. FDA menu labelling, by contrast, binds chains with 20 or more locations, not apps, and FDA has said third-party platforms are not covered establishments ([Restaurant Business](https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/food/consumer-groups-want-menu-labeling-enforced-delivery-apps)).

State privacy law turns on inference and precision. Washington's My Health My Data Act counts information "derived or extrapolated from nonhealth information… including algorithms or machine learning" as consumer health data and treats inferring as collecting ([RCW 19.373.010](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373.010)), so ranked meals tied to body goals, and allergy lists, plausibly qualify. Collection can rest on necessity for a requested service, but sharing needs consent "separate and distinct" from the consent to collect ([RCW 19.373.030](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373.030)), and the first class action under the law was filed in February 2025 ([Proskauer](https://privacylaw.proskauer.com/2025/03/articles/privacy-law/my-health-my-dollar-amazons-health-data-troubles-in-washington/)). Precise location is avoidable: Apple's own documentation uses "an app that returns a list of restaurants within driving distance" as its example of an app that "doesn't need someone's precise location" ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/corelocation/getting-the-current-location-of-a-device)), and staying approximate keeps Weight Class outside the precise-geolocation definitions of California (1,850 ft) and of Washington and Maryland (1,750 ft) ([CPPA](https://cppa.ca.gov/pdf/20260101_ccpa_statute.pdf); [RCW 19.373.010](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373.010)); Maryland allows sensitive data only when strictly necessary and does not accept consent as a basis ([Constangy](https://www.constangy.com/constangy-cyber-advisor/maryland-online-data-privacy-act-is-broader-stricter-and-more-easily-triggered-than-many-state-privacy-laws)). MenuFit, by contrast, declares precise location linked to identity. Weight Class should lead with name search, use approximate location for "near me", never store coordinates or send them to the AI, and keep menu photos, which carry location metadata ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/guide/personal-safety/manage-location-metadata-in-photos-ips0d7a5df82/web)), on the phone.

App Review adds procedure more than prohibition. Beyond 1.4.1's demand for disclosed methodology, developers report 1.4.1 rejections of diet-recommendation apps that lacked in-app citations with working links ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/742689); [thread 767340](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/767340)); 5.1.2(i) requires explicit permission before sharing personal data "with third-party AI"; 5.1.1(iv) requires an alternative such as manual entry if location is declined; and 2.3 requires metadata that reflects the real experience ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). The newest risk is Apple's 16 October 2026 update, which moved "hidden, dormant, or undocumented features" into a new guideline 2.5.19 ([Apple Developer News](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=92th7e5i)); its full text was not reviewed here, but a server-flagged feature sits squarely in its path. Weight Class's current practice, disclosing the switched-off restaurant menus in review notes and turning them on only with a later reviewed version ([PLAN.md](../PLAN.md)), is the right template, and ranking should ship the same way. The copycat rule 4.3(b) barely touches a feature inside an established tracker but would bite a standalone spin-off in a category with dozens of clones. And the one big calorie app Apple pulled in 2026, Cal AI, went for off-store payments and "deceptive billing design", not AI accuracy ([9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2026/04/21/popular-calorie-tracker-briefly-pulled-from-app-store-over-iap-and-billing-violations/)), so MenuFit's trial confusion is the part of its playbook with the most App Review exposure.

## A four-layer build answers MenuFit's top complaints

| Layer | Choice | Why | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Places | Name search first; "near me" through Apple MapKit with approximate location and stored Place IDs, or a self-hosted US subset of Overture places, whose `brand` field carries Wikidata IDs for chain matching | Storable and joinable, with no ranking ban; avoids Google's directory limits and Yelp's ranking ban | Free quota or open data |
| Chain nutrition | First-party table of the top 25–50 chains from their own published nutrition, all FDA nutrients, a source link and check date per item, and a "report wrong item" loop; FatSecret kept for unranked lookup and logging if it approves storage | The only source that allows ranking, labels and indefinite storage without licence fees | Staff time |
| Independents | Menu photo, on-device text recognition, user confirms the dish list, server AI estimates components with portion priors by restaurant type, one follow-up question, a range and an "AI estimate" label | No licensed data exists; text-only calls are cheaper than vision calls and keep photos on the phone | AI text calls |
| Ranking and logging | Deterministic scorer against what's left today with realism rules, GLP-1 tie-breakers and calm-mode defaults; one-tap logging tagged by source; the method published on the existing sources page | Fixes MenuFit's gamed picks and meets 1.4.1 | Code only |

The order of work follows the licences. First, one letter to FatSecret covering storage of logged items, what the Premier Free "Caching" tick means, and whether any edition can permit ranking; the factual feature then ships visibly in a reviewed build once the static outbound IPs are in place ([PLAN.md](../PLAN.md)). Second, compile the top-chain table, which MacroBite shows can reach 206 chains and 3,212 meals with a source link on each ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/macrobite-macros-eating-out/id6759697758)), build the scorer, and enforce a rule that ranked lists draw only from first-party rows, so FatSecret values never enter them. Third, add menu reading for independents, after running the chain golden set against the current model and extending the AI consent to menu text, photos, restaurant identity and location: Apple's `RecognizeDocumentsRequest` reads tables such as price columns on the device ([WWDC25](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/272/)), and the sibling chart app already carries a 50-line Expo module around `VNRecognizeTextRequest` (`chartme/modules/chart-text-recognition`) that can serve as the starting point. Fourth, reopen Nutritionix, or a quote for its bulk licence, only when restaurant usage justifies $22,000 or more a year. Throughout, the feature belongs inside Premium with a free preview instead of MenuFit's quiz-then-paywall ambush, the copy avoids "guilt-free", "accurate" and "safe", and MenuFit's proven content format, fast-food swap comparisons, can feed Weight Class's own top of funnel without its claims.

## Conclusion

MenuFit's success is a distribution story wearing an AI costume. Its defensible assets are a founder audience and a creator-testing machine, not data or models, which is why 41 clones have barely dented it and why Weight Class should not fight it as a standalone recommender. The structural advantage belongs to a tracker: only an app that knows what has already been logged can rank against what is left and close the loop in one tap, and the incumbents best placed to do so appear to be retreating from restaurant browsing. Inverting MenuFit's architecture, with code where published data exists and AI only where it does not, turns out to be simultaneously the more accurate, more licensable, more private and more reviewable design; the usual trade-off between product ambition and compliance does not exist here.

The decisive move is also the cheapest. A first-party chain table does triple duty as licensed ranking data, as the golden set the model benchmark lacks, and as the "from the restaurant" trust signal that MenuFit's reviewers keep asking for, which turns Nutritionix from a launch dependency into a later scale decision. The open uncertainties are real but unlikely to change the direction: MenuFit's revenue rests on third-party estimates and founder claims, the incumbents' removals on user reports, the accuracy figures on studies of plates and packaged meals rather than restaurant menus, and the reach of Washington's health-data law and the FTC's breach rule on counsel's reading, each worth checking before money is committed.
